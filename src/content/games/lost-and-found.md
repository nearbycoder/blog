It's October 1962 at Ninefold Junction, and you've just taken over the Lost Property desk from Agnes Pell, who ran it for forty-one years. Commuters come to the window to claim what they've lost. You search the drawers and shelves, turn each object over in your hands, find what's hidden inside, and stamp the claim **RETURN**, **REFUSE**, or **SEAL** it in the Iron Drawer.

Some claimants are lying. Some things hum when their owner is near. Some things come from tomorrow. And some belongings should never be returned at all, especially not to the polite grey gentleman who keeps asking for the keys.

## Looking closely is the whole game

There are no timers and no walls of text. Every case can be solved from what's on the desk: the intake tag that says where and when the object was found, the details hidden in the object itself, the claimant's story, and the rules Agnes left behind.

Pick something up and it comes to your hands under the lamp. Drag to turn it, scroll to lean in, and open its lid, latch, or clasp. A magnifier glints when you're close to a hidden detail, such as a name strip inside an umbrella or a date stamped on the back of a photograph. Each discovery is written onto the claim slip, and clicking it asks the claimant a neutral question about it. Everything said in the claim is written on a card beside the slip, so you can compare an answer with a finding without asking again.

Honest owners know what's inside. Liars only know what they could have seen from across the counter, so they bluff. The game never announces a contradiction; you compare, and the evening ledger tells you whether you were right. A stamp can't be taken back, so while you hold one over the slip, the hint says exactly what it will do there.

## Help at hand

Press H during a claim for one of **Agnes's nudges**, pinned at the side of the screen. It blocks nothing, and each press goes a step further for wherever you've got to, until the drawer lights up or a glint marks the hidden detail. Nudges never say which stamp to use. **Agnes's rules** arrive through the week as notes in her hand and live on a card beside the slip, and the **ledger book** on the desk keeps every evening's Day Ledger page as it was that evening.

## The uncanny, gently

Things arrive from other times on Platform 9. A pocket watch found tomorrow runs backwards, and anything from tomorrow goes in the Iron Drawer, even if its owner is standing in front of you. A cold visitor frosts the window glass. From Thursday, Agnes's lamp gains a blue filter that reveals hidden ink, and a ring in a velvet box turns up. Return it to the right person and every photograph on your desk changes. Mr Vell, the Grey Gentleman, knows every detail of every object, and nothing ever hums for him.

The week runs five days, with 25 cases, 25 objects, 25 sculpted characters, and three endings. Choices carry forward: refused items wait in storage for their real owner, and every gift to the Grey Gentleman visibly drains the colour from the station. Every evening the Day Ledger marks each case against the rules and explains why, and the Ninefold Gazette reports what your choices did. There are 25 optional curios, one secret per object, and a full week takes about an hour.

## Settings and graphics fidelity

Lost & Found plays with mouse or touchpad and keyboard, or a gamepad, and every menu works from the arrow keys or the d-pad with a visible focus. Settings include **Large text**, **Plain lettering** in place of handwriting on tags, slips and Agnes's notes, text speed, brightness for the scene, **Reduce motion**, screen shake, film effects, turning speed, and separate volumes. The window can be any shape, and a narrower window sees the whole desk.

**Graphics fidelity** has four steps. High is the desk as designed, with brass and glass reflecting the room from a reflection probe and dust drifting in the lamp's light. Ultra adds 1.25× supersampling with 4× MSAA, 4096 shadow maps, finer ambient occlusion, high-quality depth of field, a 64-bit colour buffer for smoother gradients in the dark, and more dust. On the integrated Radeon it was measured on, a frame took about 2.6 to 2.8 ms on Low, 4.4 to 4.7 on High and 10.4 to 11.8 on Ultra at 1600×900.

## What it's built with

Lost & Found runs on **Unity 6** with the Universal Render Pipeline. Its main scene is empty. A `Boot` script builds the whole game at runtime from code and the `Resources` folder: camera rig, lights, post-processing, desk, props, UI, and the director that runs days and cases. Restarting a day tears everything down and builds it again, which keeps scene wiring from going stale.

All content is JSON: objects, hidden details, hotspots, claims, answers, verdicts, story flags, newspaper headlines, and endings. Blender models line up with that data by name. Hotspots are empties named `HS_<detail>`, and moving parts are nodes named `Lid`, `Flap`, or `Key`.

A rules solver keeps every case fair. It sees only what a player could learn at the desk by that point in the week and must reach each case's intended verdict, or the build fails validation. The same solver powers an AutoPilot that plays the entire week through the real desk systems to each ending.

## How it was made

This was one of eight games built in parallel from short designer briefs, each by its own AI coding session on a single shared 32-core machine. The brief for this one, in full, was a sentence: _run the lost-and-found desk for a train station where some belongings should never be returned_. It came with a scope (one desk, 25 objects, a five-day story) and a trailer moment: return a wedding ring, and every photograph on your desk changes.

Everything is generated by code in the repository. Characters are sculpted as signed distance fields and meshed with marching cubes, so faces, lips, and ears are one continuous surface. They're animated procedurally, with breathing, blinking, glances, and talking, rather than rigs. The photographs on the desk are staged with the real character models in Blender, then aged in Python into 1921 sepia, 1951 silver, and 1962 Polaroid. About 170 sound effects, voices, and ambience clips come from a small NumPy synthesis toolkit, along with a swing-jazz score built around a nine-note "Ninefold" motif.

After launch, the game went through [twelve rounds of improvements](/articles/orchestrating-15-games-with-t3-code/), each run by a fresh AI session and pushed only after its logs were checked. Those rounds found two cases whose evidence couldn't be reached, and that a tap going down and up between two frames was ignored, so only 1 in 11 touchpad taps worked. They added Agnes's nudges, the transcript card, the stamp hint, the rules card and ledger book, curiosities, gamepad play and keyboard menus, a crash-safe save, accessibility settings, windows of any shape, reflections and dust, and the fidelity slider. The suite runs 136 tests, and a whole week has been played through the compositor's real pointer and keyboard inside a private, invisible KWin desktop.

The trailer was shot by the game itself. A frame-locked recorder runs at 30 fps at Ultra, pipes frames to FFmpeg, and captures the mixed audio, and its play mode drives a simulated mouse and keyboard through whole days. The project was developed with [Claude Code](https://claude.com/claude-code).
